In Alibaug and the Western Ghats foothills, you often encounter a mix of black cotton soil (vertisol) and laterite (ferrallitic soil). These two soils behave oppositely. Black cotton soil has a high shrink-swell potential. When it dries, it shrinks. When it rains, it swells. This movement can lift foundations by 50mm or more. This is catastrophic for rigid structures. Laterite, on the other hand, is stable. It does not swell. It is a natural aggregate. But it is dense. It does not absorb water quickly. It needs aeration.</p><p>Black cotton soil contains montmorillonite clay. This mineral has a high cation exchange capacity. It holds onto water and nutrients tightly. But it also holds onto toxins. If your land was used for intensive agriculture, this clay is saturated with nitrates and phosphates. You cannot just add compost. You must dilute and aerate. The shrink-swell data for vertisol in this region is critical. A 10% change in moisture content can cause a 2% volume change. For a 10-meter foundation, that is 200mm of movement. Your walls will crack. Your floors will lift. You must design for this movement or eliminate it.</p><p>Laterite soil in Goa and coastal Alibaug is different. It is rich in iron oxide and aluminum silicates. It is red or yellow. It is hard when dry, soft when wet. It has low shrink-swell potential. But it has low infiltration rate. Water sits on top. This leads to waterlogging. You must create channels for water to move. You must break the hardpan. You must add organic matter to create pore space. The goal is to turn laterite into a sponge, not a rock. This requires specific earthworks. It requires time. It requires patience.</p><h2>Measuring Infiltration: The 100mm Rainfall Test</h2><p>You need to know how fast your soil drinks water. This is the infiltration rate. It is measured in millimeters per hour (mm/hr). Compacted, chemical-damaged soil in Alibaug often has an infiltration rate of 2-5 mm/hr. This is dangerously low. A 100mm rainfall event (a heavy monsoon burst) will overwhelm this soil. The water will run off. It will erode your topsoil. It will flood your basement. Regenerated soil, with high organic matter and aeration, can achieve 20-50 mm/hr. This is a 10x improvement. This is the difference between a disaster and a garden.</p><p>How do you measure this? You do a percolation test. Dig a hole 300mm deep and 300mm wide. Fill it with water. Let it soak in. Measure the time it takes for the water level to drop by 100mm. This is your percolation time. Convert this to mm/hr. If it takes 10 hours to drop 100mm, your rate is 10 mm/hr. This is still low. You need to improve it. You need to add compost. You need to aerate. You need to plant. The goal is to get below 5 hours for a 100mm drop. This is a healthy, regenerative soil. It can handle the monsoon. It can support your villa.</p><p>Consider a 100-meter swale. If your swale is 1m wide and 0.5m deep, it holds 50,000 liters. You need two swales, or a larger one. This is the math. You cannot guess. You must calculate. This is where most architects fail. Shake it hard for 5 minutes. Let it sit for 24 hours. You will see three layers. The bottom layer is sand. The middle layer is silt. The top layer is clay. Measure the height of each layer.
