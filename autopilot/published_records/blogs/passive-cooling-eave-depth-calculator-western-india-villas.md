<h2>How Do You Calculate the Exact Eave Depth for 100% Summer Shade?</h2>
<p>The solar altitude angle changes drastically between Alibaug (18.5°N) and Gujarat (23°N). At 18.5°N, the sun is higher in the sky during summer. To block the sun at its peak (12:00–14:00), you need a deeper overhang than you would in a temperate zone. The formula is simple: Eave Depth = Wall Height / Tan(Solar Altitude Angle).</p>

<p>For a standard 3-meter high wall in Alibaug, the solar altitude at noon in June is approximately 75 degrees. The tangent of 75 degrees is 3.73. Therefore, the required eave depth is 3m / 3.73 = 0.8 meters. However, this only blocks the direct beam. To account for diffuse radiation and the sun’s path from 12:00 to 14:00, we increase the depth. A 1.8-meter eave is the empirical sweet spot for Western India coastal villas. It blocks the harsh afternoon sun while allowing winter sun to penetrate for passive heating.</p>

<p>In Gujarat, the latitude is 23°N. The sun is slightly lower. A 1.5-meter eave might suffice for the same wall height. But in the humid coastal zones of Goa and Alibaug, the 1.8-meter depth is non-negotiable. It creates a 'shadow buffer' that keeps the wall temperature down. This is not a guess. It is trigonometry applied to your specific plot.</p>

<h2>Why Does a 0.25m Rammed Earth Wall Outperform RCC in Thermal Lag?</h2>
<p>Thermal mass is the ability of a material to absorb and store heat. Standard Reinforced Cement Concrete (RCC) is 0.15m thick. It has low thermal mass. It heats up quickly in the morning and releases that heat into your living space in the evening. This is why your house is hottest at 6 PM, not at noon.</p>

<p>A 0.25m thick rammed earth wall has significantly higher thermal mass. The density of rammed earth is approximately 2,000 kg/m³. The specific heat capacity is 0.8 kJ/kg·K. This combination creates a 4-6 hour lag in heat transfer. The wall absorbs the heat from the morning sun and releases it slowly into the ground or the night air, not into your room.</p>

It stores the heat and discharges it when the outside air is cooler.</p>

<p>For coastal zones like Alibaug, the humidity adds another layer of complexity. Rammed earth walls are breathable. They allow moisture vapor to pass through, preventing the condensation issues that plague sealed RCC walls. This breathability is critical for long-term durability in a 3,200mm monsoon climate.</p>

<h2>How Do You Size a Swale to Handle a 100-Year Rainfall Event?</h2>
<p>In Alibaug and Goa, the 100-year rainfall event is approximately 200mm per hour. For a 5,000 sq. ft. (465 m²) plot, that is 93,000 liters of water hitting your land in one hour. If you do not manage this water, it will flood your basement or erode your foundation.</p>

<p>A vegetated swale is the most effective way to manage this water. The swale is a shallow, vegetated channel that slows down and infiltrates the water. The sizing math is based on the flow rate and the infiltration rate of your soil. For laterite soil, the infiltration rate is approximately 10mm per hour. You need a swale volume that can hold the excess water until it infiltrates.</p>

<p>The formula for swale volume is: Volume = (Rainfall Intensity × Catchment Area × Time) - (Infiltration Rate × Swale Area × Time). For a 5,000 sq. ft. plot, a swale that is 1 meter wide and 0.5 meters deep can hold 250 cubic meters of water. This is enough to handle the peak flow of a 100-year event. The swale should be placed on the contour, following the natural slope of the land.</p>

<h2>What Are the Field Tests to Verify Your Soil’s Infiltration Rate?</h2>
<p>You cannot design a swale without knowing your soil’s infiltration rate. The standard test is the 24-Hour Soil Jar Test. Fill a 1-liter jar with soil from your site. Add 1 liter of water. Stir vigorously for 1 minute. Let it settle for 24 hours. Measure the layers of sand, silt, and clay.</p>

<p>For a swale to work effectively, you need a soil with at least 30% sand and 20% silt. Clay should be less than 30%. If your soil is more than 30% clay, the infiltration rate will be too low, and the swale will flood. In this case, you need to amend the soil with sand or create a larger swale volume.</p>

<p>The 15mm Soil Sausage Test is another quick field test. Take a handful of moist soil and roll it into a sausage. If the sausage breaks at 15mm, your soil is sandy. If it bends around a finger without breaking, your soil is clayey. This test gives you a quick estimate of your soil’s plasticity and infiltration potential.</p>
