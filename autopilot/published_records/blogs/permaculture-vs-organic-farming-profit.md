<h2>1. What is profitability in this context?</h2>
<p>Profitability means net cash flow per hectare per year after all variable and fixed costs, plus ecosystem services monetised. In India we use ₹150 k/ha/yr as the baseline for a decent organic vegetable plot.</p>

<h3>Why it matters</h3>
<p>Net cash flow tells you what you actually pocket, not just what you sell. It includes tourism, carbon credits, and water‑banking – all the extra streams that most farmers ignore.</p>

<h3>What most people get wrong</h3>
<p>Many compare only crop sales and forget that organic farms often rely on seasonal tourism or niche markets to stay afloat.</p>

<h3>What no one talks about</h3>
<p>The value of ecosystem services is rarely priced in the market. Carbon credits, biodiversity offsets, and water‑banking can add ₹50 k/ha or more.</p>

<h2>4. Labour comparison – the hidden cost of hands</h2>
<p>Organic veg: ~30 person‑days/ha/yr. Permaculture: ~9 person‑days/ha/yr.</p>

<h3>Why it matters</h3>
<p>Labour costs rise with wages. In Uttarakhand, ₹400/day is the average.</p>

<h3>Why most people get it wrong</h3>
<p>They assume labour is a fixed cost and ignore the seasonal spikes.</p>

<h3>What no one talks about</h3>
<p>Perennial systems reduce labour by 70 % after establishment, freeing up time for other income activities.</p>

<h3>How to apply it</h3>
<p>Use a labour audit tool to track days per hectare. Compare the numbers before and after you introduce a food‑forest guild.</p>

<h2>5. Water usage profile – a cost‑saving lever</h2>
<p>Organic veg: 12 000 L/ha/yr. Permaculture: 4 500 L/ha/yr.</p>

<h3>Why it works</h3>

<h3>Why most people get it wrong</h3>

<h3>What no one talks about</h3>
<p>Water savings translate to ₹2 200/ha and also improve resilience during drought.</p>
